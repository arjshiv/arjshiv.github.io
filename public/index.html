Arjun Kannan, co-founder of ResiDesk

The person doing the work usually knows what's wrong.

I first learned that in a Cornell physics lab. A researcher was spending hours checking magnetic noise by hand, so I turned an iPhone compass into a faster way to do it. The program was small. Watching someone get part of his day back changed what I wanted to build.

What I'm building ↘ Say hello ↗

Since then I have built software for financial advisors at BlackRock, led product, engineering, and data at Climb Credit, and started ResiDesk. The industries changed. I still work the same way. I find the person closest to the problem, ask what the software is missing, and keep fixing it until the work gets easier.

ResiDesk

A resident should not have to explain the same problem three times.

A broken lock starts in a text. The resident mentions it again on a call. A week later it shows up in a review because nobody could see the whole story.

Property teams are not ignoring residents. They are trying to work across inboxes, property systems, spreadsheets, vendors, and policies that change by building. The information exists, but it arrives in pieces.

That is why we built ResiDesk. We help teams answer residents, see what is still open, and notice when the same problem keeps coming back. I spend most of my time on product and data, but that is only the neat description. I also sell, hire, chase bugs, sit in customer calls, and help wherever the company is stuck.

AI is good at reading a lot of messy history. It is also good at sounding sure when it is wrong. In housing, the answer has to fit the lease, the building, and what already happened. When the software is unsure, a person has to take over.

If twenty residents misunderstand the move-in instructions, write better instructions. Do not automate the twentieth apology.

How I got here

I thought I was going to be a physicist.

Both my parents have PhDs, and I thought I would get one too. Then a lab script made software feel more immediate than a paper. The rest was less of a plan.

Physics

An iPhone compass got me into software.

Our microscope was sensitive to electromagnetic noise. Checking the room took a lot of manual work. The phone already had a magnetometer, so I wrote an app that cut the setup time. That was enough.

BlackRock

BlackRock rejected me. Six months later, I asked again.

The interview had felt like solving a real problem together, so I thanked the team and stayed in touch. When another role opened, I tried again. Then I asked if they could move it from Delaware to New York. Luck mattered. So did asking without turning it into an ultimatum. Once there, I won the internal hackathon three times. The first prototype looked clever and went nowhere because a spreadsheet was easier. The later work became software advisors used to explain portfolios to clients.

Climb Credit

The loan had to answer a harder question.

At Climb Credit, we financed career-focused education. A credit score could tell us whether someone had repaid a loan before. It could not tell us whether the school would help them finish, find work, or earn more. I ran product, engineering, and data while we built those outcomes into the way we made loans.

How I work

I like people who tell me when I am wrong. It saves a lot of time.

01

Start with the person stuck with the problem.

Ask what they were trying to finish. Then ask what the software made them remember, copy, chase, or explain again.

02

Take the complaint seriously. Then count.

Someone can be right that a problem hurts and wrong that it happens everywhere. Thank them for finding it. Then count the cases and fix the cause.

03

Make it work on the busy day.

The demo is over. The queue is full. The policy is unclear. Nobody who built the thing is in the room. That is the test.

04

Do not make someone fix the same mistake twice.

A person will always catch things the software misses. If they catch the same mistake twice, we failed to learn from the first one.

04

Writing

I write when an idea sounds a little too neat.

Talking lets you skate past the weak parts. Writing does not. I usually start after I hear myself say something in a meeting that sounds right but may not be.

Most of what I write about now is AI. The model is rarely the whole product. It needs the right context, tools, tests, and a clean way to hand the work back to a person. A demo can hide all of that.

Fair housing is the harder edge of the same question. If software helps decide who gets an answer, which problem gets attention, or when a person steps in, we should be able to test whether it treats people fairly.
